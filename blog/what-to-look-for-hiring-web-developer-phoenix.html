What to look for when hiring a web developer in Phoenix

Nov 1, 2026·7 min read·Jeel Kakadiya

If you run a small business in Phoenix, Tempe, Scottsdale, Mesa, or anywhere across the Valley, your website is often the first thing a new customer sees. Hiring the right person to build it should not feel like a gamble. But most owners have never done it before, so they go on price alone, pick the cheapest quote, and end up paying twice. This is a plain guide to choosing well the first time, written for people who do not speak code.

You are not really buying a website. You are buying a result: more calls, more bookings, more trust. Hire for that.

Does hiring someone local actually matter?

A little, and in specific ways. The web is global, and plenty of great work gets done remotely, so do not rule out someone just because they are not in Arizona. But a local developer does bring real advantages for a small business. You can meet face to face, you share a timezone so replies are same-day, and someone who knows the Phoenix market understands your customers, your competitors, and the local search terms people actually type. If showing up in Google for “near me” searches matters to you, that local context is worth something.

The questions to ask before you hire

You do not need to understand the answers in technical detail. You need to hear clear, confident, jargon-free explanations. If someone cannot explain their work to you simply, they will be just as hard to work with later.

Ask every candidate these
  • Can I see work you have done for a business like mine? Real, live sites, not just screenshots or templates.
  • Who owns the website and the code when we are done? The answer should be you, with full access to your domain and accounts.
  • What happens after launch? Updates, backups, security, and who to call when something breaks.
  • How will people find this site on Google? They should mention speed, mobile, and local search, not promise a magic number-one ranking.
  • What do you need from me, and by when? A good developer manages the project, not just the code.
  • What is not included in this price? Get the line between the quote and the extras in writing.

If the answers are vague, slow, or full of jargon, that is your answer. Move on.

Agency, freelancer, or do it yourself

There are three common ways to get a website built, and none of them is wrong. They just fit different budgets and different needs. Here is the honest trade-off.

Green flags and red flags

After the first call, you usually know more than you think. Trust the pattern. These are the signals that matter more than a polished pitch.

Green flags
  • Asks about your business and customers before talking tech.
  • Shows live work and real results, not just pretty mockups.
  • Explains things simply, without making you feel behind.
  • Puts scope, price, and timeline in writing.
  • Is clear that you own everything at the end.
  • Replies when they say they will.
Red flags
  • A price with no written scope behind it.
  • Guarantees the number-one spot on Google.
  • Keeps your domain or logins under their control.
  • Dodges simple questions with jargon.
  • Slow or vague before you have even paid.
  • No plan for what happens after launch.

What a good process looks like

A website is not a single handoff. A developer who works well will walk you through a clear path, roughly like this:

  • Discovery. They learn your business, your goals, and what a win looks like before quoting anything.
  • A written plan. Pages, features, timeline, and price, so there are no surprises.
  • Design you approve. You see the look and feel before a line of code is written.
  • Build and review. They build it, you check it on your own phone and laptop, and changes are made.
  • Launch and handoff. It goes live, you get every login, and you know who to call.

A quick word on price

Cheap websites are rarely cheap. A $300 site that nobody can find, that you cannot edit, and that breaks in six months costs far more than it saved. At the same time, you do not need to overspend. The right number depends on what the site actually has to do. I broke the real ranges down in a separate post on what a small business should budget, and if you want to understand what quietly drives customers away once the site is live, see why your website might be losing customers.

Judge a quote by what it includes, not just the number at the bottom. The cheapest bid is often the most expensive one.

The honest bottom line

Hire the person who understands your business first and the technology second, who explains things plainly, who puts it in writing, and who hands you the keys at the end. If they happen to know the Phoenix market too, even better. That combination is worth far more than the lowest quote.
