How much should a small business budget for custom software?

Oct 18, 2026·7 min read·Jeel Kakadiya

Everybody wants to know the number, and nobody wants to say it first. You have a problem worth solving, you suspect software can fix it, and the only thing standing between you and a conversation is the fear that the answer is “more than you can afford.” So let us take the mystery out of it. There is no single price, but there are real ranges, and a simple way to think about where your project lands.

The right question is not “what does software cost.” It is “what is this problem costing me, and is solving it worth that?”

The rough ranges

These are ballpark figures for scoped project work with a freelancer or small studio, not a big agency with a sales team to pay for. Every project is different, but most fall into one of four buckets.

Typical budget ranges by project size
$500 to $3k
Automations & integrations
Connect two tools, auto-sync data, or remove a manual copy-paste step.
$3k to $15k
Internal tools & dashboards
A focused app for your team: a tracker, a reporting dashboard, a simple workflow.
$15k to $50k
Custom web applications
A real product with accounts, a database, multiple roles, and outside integrations.
$50k+
Larger platforms & ongoing builds
Multi-part systems, heavy scale or compliance, built and maintained over time.

Treat these as starting points, not quotes. The same idea can sit in two different buckets depending on how much it actually needs to do.

What actually drives the price

Two things decide where you land: how much there is to build, and how certain you are about what you want. The first one is obvious. The second one is where most budgets quietly blow up.

Pushes the price up
  • Several user types with different permissions.
  • Integrations with other systems and tools.
  • Polished, custom-designed interfaces.
  • Security, compliance, or sensitive data.
  • Migrating messy existing data.
  • Scope that is unclear or keeps changing.
Keeps the price down
  • One clear core workflow to solve first.
  • Proven building blocks instead of custom everything.
  • Clean, well-organized existing data.
  • Flexibility on the nice-to-haves.
  • A phased build you can pause between.
  • Decisions made up front, before code.

The single biggest cost driver is not a feature. It is uncertainty. Every “we will figure that out later” turns into rework, and rework is the most expensive line item there is. The clearer you are about the one problem you want solved, the less you pay to solve it.

Buy, build, or extend

Most budgeting mistakes come from comparing the wrong things. A ready-made tool looks cheaper than a custom build because you are comparing a monthly fee to a one-time cost. The fair comparison is total cost over time, and whether you own the result.

The three options, honestly compared
ApproachUpfrontOngoingYou own it
Off-the-shelf toolLowPer seat, every month, foreverNo, you rent it
Extend what you haveModerateLowPartly
Custom buildHigherLow (mostly hosting)Yes

Off-the-shelf usually wins early. Custom starts to pay off when per-seat fees grow with your team, or the tool fights the way you work.

If your process is standard and your team is small, a subscription is often the smart call. Custom earns its higher upfront cost when the way you work is your advantage, or when renting the same tool for years would cost more than owning one. If you are weighing this for your customer data specifically, I wrote a whole post on custom CRM vs. off-the-shelf.

How to make your budget go further

  • Start with one workflow, not the whole dream. Build the most painful part first and let it prove its value before you spend more.
  • Use proven building blocks. A managed database and existing login tools cost far less than building those from scratch.
  • Get the scope clear before anyone writes code. An hour of deciding saves days of rebuilding.
  • Build in phases. A good project has natural stopping points, so you can bank the value and decide what is next.

A simple way to decide if it is worth it

Before you worry about the price, estimate the cost of the problem. Roughly how many hours a week does your team lose to it? Multiply that by a loaded hourly cost and by 50 weeks. That is what the problem costs you every year, before you count the errors, the slow responses, and the burnout that are harder to put a number on.

If a tool saves your team ten hours a week, it pays for itself faster than almost anything else you will buy this year.

When the yearly cost of the problem is larger than the cost to fix it, the decision usually makes itself. When it is not, that is useful too: it tells you to buy something off the shelf, or to leave it alone for now.

The honest bottom line

Ranges are a starting point, not a quote. The only way to get a real number is to scope the actual problem: what it needs to do, what it connects to, and where it can stay simple.
